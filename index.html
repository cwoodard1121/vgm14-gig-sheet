      Reference

      Mid-set troubleshootingFeedback, vanishing vocals, dead channels

      You'll be holding a guitar. These are all one move.

      Feedback, right now
      Main fader down 3 dB first, then find the channel. Don't start twisting EQ mid-song.
      Vocals disappearing
      It's the guitars, not the vocals. Pull guitars 2 dB before you push vocals up.
      You can't hear yourself
      Zone level knob. Not your guitar volume, and not the master.
      Drummer is signalling you
      He can't hear vocals, and he has no knobs of his own. MON fader — one fader, and it doesn't touch the room.
      Muddy once the room fills
      Bodies absorb high end. Pull 250 Hz on the graphic rather than boosting treble.
      Something's distorting
      Solo it and watch the meter before you touch a fader. A channel clipping at the preamp won't be fixed downstream.
      A channel died
      Cable first, always. You have three spare mic cables — that's why.
      MON out fails entirely
      Move the cable to Aux out, switch Aux to pre, turn up the same six channels. Five minutes, and the bus is already sitting there.

      Tape your faders before doors

      Gaff tape along the fader bed and a marker line at each position the moment soundcheck is done — MON fader included. You will bump something in the dark, and reconstructing a mix from memory while playing a song is not a thing anyone does well.

      EQ by symptomWhat to cut when it sounds wrong

      You have one sweepable mid per channel. Sweep with a wide ear, find the offender, then cut it.

      It sounds…Look atMove
      Boomy, muddy, one note200–350 HzCut. The number one small-PA problem
      Boxy, like cardboard400–600 HzCut 4–6 dB. Toms and kick almost always need this
      Honky, nasal, a "wah" quality800 Hz–1 kHzCut 3–4 dB
      Buried, can't make out words2–4 kHzBoost 2–3 dB
      Harsh, brittle, icepick3–5 kHzCut. Classic SM57 on a bright amp
      Dull, no air on top10–12 kHzHigh shelf up. It's a 12 kHz shelf, so it adds air, not presence
      Rumble, stage thumps, handling noisebelow 80 HzEngage the HPF. Free, and it fixes more than any boost

      The rule for the night

      Cut to fix, boost to flavour. Every boost spends headroom you don't have much of — two 12s and a mixer are doing a lot of work already. When you catch yourself reaching for a boost, first try cutting whatever is covering the thing you wanted louder.

      One more on the high shelf: it sits at 12 kHz, which is air, not presence. If a vocal needs to cut through, the move is the sweepable mid at 3 kHz, not the high shelf. Use the shelf on overheads and vocals for sheen and leave it alone on close-mic'd guitar cabs, where it mostly adds hiss.

      Remember channel EQ is shared: it feeds the mains and the drummer's MON mix, because MON is tapped after the EQ. A cut that fixes a vocal out front also fixes it in his wedge, which is handy — but a big boost you make purely for the room lands in his monitor too.

      Fixing what went wrong out frontWhy the drums vanished last time

      On stage the drums were fine. Out front they weren't — because the kit was the only thing in the band not coming out of a speaker.

      That's the actual diagnosis, and it's a different problem from the one it looks like. Everything else — both guitars, both vocals — arrived at the audience at full strength from two boxes at the front of the stage. The kit arrived acoustically, from well upstage, having lost level the whole way and having bounced off the back wall on the way out. From where you were standing it sounded balanced. Twenty feet out it wasn't close.

      Two independent fixes, and you're doing both this time:

      Put the kit in the PA
      That's what the six mics are for. Once kick and snare are coming out of the same speakers as everything else, they arrive with the same authority instead of fighting a distance handicap. This is the main fix.
      Move the kit downstage
      Cheap and worth doing anyway. Distance costs roughly 6 dB every time it doubles, and the further upstage the kit is the more of what reaches the room is back-wall reflection rather than the kit itself. Get him as far forward as the stage allows.

      Worth knowing what to expect: the mics won't make the kit louder on stage — it's already the loudest thing up there. What they do is make the kick punch and the snare crack land out front. So don't chase "more drums" by pushing all six faders up. Push kick and snare; the toms and overheads are seasoning.

      And your monitoring problem was a separate thing entirely — no wedge, so you were stepping out in front of the PA to hear anything. The Zone feed solves that one, and it means you'd have caught this drum problem yourself last time.

      Placement, in order of how much it matters

      Match the overhead distances. This is the big one.
      Use a mic cable as a tape measure: run it from the centre of the snare to one overhead capsule, pinch it, then check the other one is the same length. If the distances don't match, the snare arrives at each mic at a different moment and partially cancels — and a mic'd kit that sounds smaller than the acoustic kit is almost always this. Also keep each overhead at least three times as far from the snare as the snare mic is (the 3:1 rule).
      Angle the snare mic away from the hi-hat
      1–2" in from the rim, over the top head, pointed at the centre, back turned to the hats. There are no gates on this board, so whatever the snare mic hears is in the mix all night.
      Kick mic at the beater, not the middle
      Two to four inches inside the port, aimed at the spot where the beater strikes. That's where click comes from — you're spending the mid band on a 375 Hz cut instead, so placement has to supply the attack.
      Check the tom clips against his swing
      Rim clips put the mic right in the stick path. Have him play the fills he actually plays before you settle them, not a polite roll.
      SM57 halfway out on the Katana's cone
      Right up on the grille, an inch or two off, aimed halfway between the dust cap and the edge. Dead centre is bright and harsh, the edge is dark, halfway is the safe start.

      If you run short on time or headroom

      Pull the tom mics and one overhead. Kick, snare and a single centred overhead is a completely legitimate three-mic kit and it's far harder to get wrong than six mics. Six open drum mics on a loud stage with no gates is a lot of accumulated bleed, and bleed is what turns a mix to mud. Add the toms back only if the kit still has room to breathe.

      Gain staging & ringing outPFL, the VU meter and the graphic

      Gain staging, properly

      This board has PFL solo on every channel and a VU meter, so you don't have to guess. Solo the channel, ask for their loudest, and set the gain so the meter peaks around 0 — touching the top, not living there. Do it with the channel fader down. Gain sets how hard the signal hits the board; the fader sets how much of it goes to the room. Confusing those two is the most common reason a mix ends up thin and noisy.

      Ringing out the mains

      With the band quiet, open a vocal mic and slowly push the main fader until it starts to ring. Find that frequency on the 9-band graphic, pull the slider down 3–6 dB, repeat two or three times, then stop. Past three notches you're making the PA sound worse than the feedback was.

      Identifying the ring by ear: a low howl is 125–250 Hz; a hollow "oooo" is 500 Hz–1 kHz; a hard ring is 2–4 kHz; a piercing whistle is 8 kHz and up.

      The drummer's wedgeMON out into the Kustom FX return

      Straight into the FX Return, so the vocals never touch a guitar preamp. That's the right call, and it changes what you can adjust once you're playing.

      What the FX Return actually gets you

      It lands after the preamp and the whole tone stack, essentially just hitting the power amp and speaker. No guitar voicing, no preamp colour on the vocals — which is the entire point. The trade is that the amp's Volume, Bass, Mid, Treble and DFX knobs are all upstream of where you're plugging in, so none of them do anything. The drummer has no controls at all. Your MON fader is his only volume, and you have no EQ on his feed either, since the graphic is assigned to Main.

      So you shape his mix with send levels, not EQ

      This is the part that changes with the tone stack out of the picture. A 100-watt guitar speaker has no useful low end, and anything low you send it is wasted power and flap. Your HPFs are already doing most of that work — MON is tapped after the channel EQ and HPF, so every channel with its filter in arrives already cleaned up. The one channel that doesn't is the bass, so keep its MON send moderate rather than maxed and let the 800 Hz boost on that channel carry it. Upper harmonics are what survive a guitar speaker anyway; he'll hear the bass line fine.

      Do this and it works twice as well

      Put the amp on a chair or a road case, aimed across the kit at his head. This matters more than ever going into the FX Return: you get a fixed amount of power and no way to add gain at the amp, so the only lever left is getting the sound to his ears instead of his shins. Aiming it across the kit rather than up also keeps vocal bleed out of the overheads.

      If it isn't loud enough

      With the MON fader up and the channel sends up, what you've got is what you've got — there's no gain stage left at the amp. If that ceiling is too low, the fallback is moving the cable to the Rhythm channel's guitar input, which has preamp gain and hands the drummer his own Volume back. You lose some clarity to the preamp, and you'd want the amp's Bass near 9 o'clock, Mid at 1–2 o'clock, Treble around noon, boost switch off and DFX at zero. Try the FX Return first; for cue-level vocals it should be plenty.

      Set expectations with him either way: it will sound thin. It's a guitar speaker rolling off somewhere above 5 kHz. It's a cue wedge, not a mix. Vocals and bass will be audible and that is the entire job.

      Where every output goesSignal flow, Zone, MON and AUX

      This board has more routing than it looks like from the front: three separate send buses per channel plus a fourth output taken off the main mix. Both monitors are covered and you still have a spare.

      The move that fixes your monitoring

      The Zone/Sub output is a full-range mono sum of the main mix, tapped after the main fader, with its own level knob. Send it to your wedge and you are hearing exactly what the room is getting, at whatever volume you want, with nothing to set up and nothing to keep in sync. Last gig you had no wedge at all and were stepping out in front of the PA to check the mix — this is the fix, and it costs you zero channels and zero sends.

      Why the drummer gets MON and not AUX

      MON is permanently pre-fader, which is exactly what you want for a cue mix — nothing you do to the room mix can disturb it. AUX is the switchable one, which makes it the more flexible spare. MON also has a dedicated fader in the master section, so the drummer's overall level is a single fader you can find without looking, instead of a small knob. That matters a lot given he has no volume control of his own.

      Why you get Zone and not a real send

      Zone is the only output that mirrors the main mix automatically. Any of the three sends would mean building a second mix by hand and then keeping it in sync with the first one all night, while playing guitar. Zone costs nothing and is more accurate than anything you'd build by hand.

      The graphic EQ serves one bus or the other

      The 9-band graphic has an Assign switch: Main or Mon. It cannot do both. Set it to MAIN — ringing out the room is what saves your show, and the mains are what the audience hears. The consequence is that the drummer's wedge gets no graphic EQ at all, which matters more than usual now that you're going into the FX Return. The drummer section covers how to shape his feed without it.

      Three things to know before you're standing there

      Zone is post-master
      Pull the main fader down between songs and your wedge dies with it. Use the Zone knob, not the master, when you want your own level to change.
      Your wedge EQ lives on the speaker
      With the graphic assigned to Main, you have no board EQ on the Zone feed. The YXL10P has a Monitor voicing mode on its back panel — that's your wedge EQ, and it's built for exactly this.
      Check the Zone tap in ten seconds at soundcheck
      Sources disagree on whether Zone is taken before or after the graphic. Pull one graphic slider to the floor and listen to your wedge: if it changes, Zone is post-graphic and your wedge follows the room EQ. Either way it works, but it's worth knowing which.
      Input listMics, DI, stands, phantom

      Every mic lands on a mono channel, because only the mono channels have the 80 Hz high-pass filter and the sweepable mid. Your guitar goes on the stereo pair, where a direct signal with a cab sim misses neither.

      ChSourceMic / DIIntoStand48VHPF

      Phantom power is global on this board

      One switch puts 48 V on every XLR input. Harmless to the SM57s, SM58s and Apex dynamics — all balanced. Two rules anyway: flip it only with the master fader down, and never plug or unplug a mic while it's live. This is also why your G3Xn goes in on a 1/4" jack, not XLR — an unbalanced line source on a phantom-powered XLR input is asking for trouble.

      If the bassist's amp turns out to have an XLR DI out, use it instead of the SM57 — more consistent, and it frees a mic and a stand. Engage its ground lift if it has one.

      Stage plotKit downstage, mains in front of the mics

      Two things this drawing exists to show: the mains sit downstage of every vocal mic, and the kit comes forward from where it was last time.

      Bring the kit downstage
      This is the direct fix for last gig. Put the drummer as far forward as the stage and the band will tolerate — level in and level out, every doubling of distance costs about 6 dB, and the further back he is the more the back wall smears what does arrive. The mics do most of the work now, but free level is still free.
      Mains high on the SKS-09B stands
      Tweeter roughly at standing head height, aimed into the room. That's the whole reason for stands — up there the sound clears the front row instead of cooking it.
      Your wedge angled up at your head
      On the floor at your feet. If the YXL10P has a monitor angle moulded into one side, lay it on that side. Set its voicing mode to Monitor.
      Put your SM58's dead spot on the wedge
      A cardioid SM58 is deafest directly behind itself. Face the mic with the wedge between you and the audience firing back up at you, and the wedge sits in the null. That geometry is what lets a wedge get loud at all.
      Mixer at your position, within arm's reach
      On a table or a road case beside you — not out front. You're mixing while playing; if you can't reach it between songs it may as well be in the van.
