      Load-in

      Outdoor show, in order. Most of these steps exist to stop a later one from going wrong — tap a row to tick it.

        Board

        The whole VGM14 set to the tape — where every knob and fader starts before the first song, in the numbers printed on the panel. Tap it, or turn the phone sideways, to see it full screen.

        Master section

        • MON fader −6. The drummer's volume all night, and his only one. Raise it to taste once he's playing.
        • Zone/Sub 3. Your wedge level. Your knob all night — not MAIN.
        • MAIN 0. Mix with the channel faders, not the master.
        • Equalizer on MAIN: 125 Hz +2, the rest 0. The outdoor start — a little of the low end walls would have given back. Only notch what actually rings.
        • EFX Return to MAIN −6. This is the per-song reverb: Dry −12, Normal −6, Wet −3. Preset 6, Plate Reverb – Vocals. EFX Send 5, EFX to MON 0.
        • Phantom on — the rear switch. One 48 V switch on the back panel, for the two overheads. Flip it only with MAIN down.
        • Aux Send 0 · STEREO Return −∞. The Aux bus is a spare held in reserve; nothing comes back on the stereo return.
        • BREAK off. Press it between sets: every input mutes and the faders stay where they are.
        • Media Output 5, Source USB. Record the set and listen back. Best mixing lesson you'll get.
        Channel notes

          Panning: everything centre except the overheads, which get a modest spread. Hard-panning with two point-source mains just means half the audience misses whatever you panned away from them.

          Reference

          Mid-set troubleshootingFeedback, wind, power, rain, dead channels

          You'll be holding a guitar, outside. These are all one move.

          Feedback, right now
          MAIN from 0 down to −3 first, then find the channel. Don't start twisting EQ mid-song. Outdoors it's usually a wedge, not the mains.
          Vocals disappearing
          It's the guitars, not the vocals. Gtr 1 and Gtr 2 from 0 down to −2 before you push Vox 1 past +3.
          You can't hear yourself
          Zone/Sub knob. Not your guitar volume, and not MAIN.
          Drummer is signalling you
          He can't hear vocals, and he has no knobs of his own. MON fader — one fader, and it doesn't touch the mains.
          Thin, no low end out front
          Normal outdoors: there are no walls to hand the bass back. Kick and bass faders up first, or 125 Hz on the Equalizer from +2 to +4. Leave 63 Hz alone — the 12s don't go there.
          A low roar on the vocal mics
          Wind across the grille. Windscreens on, HPF in, and turn the mic's back to the wind if the stand allows.
          The PA drops out or starts to hum
          Power. Something else landed on your circuit — coolers, heaters, a food truck — or a light extension is sagging. MAIN down, then follow the cord to the outlet.
          Rain
          MAIN down, speakers off, cover on the board. Powered speakers and water don't mix, and no song is worth it.
          Board or phone in full sun
          Move it into shade — a mixer cooks and a phone dims itself to save its battery. Gig mode's Day setting is the high-contrast one.
          Something's distorting
          Solo it and watch the Clip LED and the VU meter before you touch a fader. A channel clipping at the preamp won't be fixed downstream.
          A channel died
          Cable first, always. You have three spare mic cables — that's why.
          MON out fails entirely
          Move the cable to Aux out, switch Aux to Pre, and turn up the same channels' Aux knobs. Five minutes, and the bus is already sitting there.

          Tape your faders before doors

          Gaff tape along the fader bed and a marker line at each position the moment soundcheck is done — MON fader included. You will bump something once it's dark, and reconstructing a mix from memory while playing a song is not a thing anyone does well.

          EQ by symptomWhat to move when it sounds wrong outside

          You have one sweepable Mid per mono channel. Sweep with a wide ear, find the offender, then cut it. Outdoors the list changes order: thin beats boomy.

          The rule for the night

          Cut to fix, boost to flavour. Every boost spends headroom you don't have much of — two 12s outdoors are already working hard. When you catch yourself reaching for a boost, first try cutting whatever is covering the thing you wanted louder.

          One more on the High knob: it's a shelf at 12 kHz, which is air, not presence. If a vocal needs to cut through, the move is the Mid swept to 3K, not the High. Use the shelf on overheads and vocals for sheen and leave it alone on close-mic'd guitar cabs, where it mostly adds hiss.

          Remember channel EQ is shared: it feeds the mains and the drummer's MON mix, because Mon is tapped after the EQ. A cut that fixes a vocal out front also fixes it in his wedge, which is handy — but a big boost you make purely for the crowd lands in his monitor too.

          Fixing what went wrong out frontWhy the drums vanished last time

          On stage the drums were fine. Out front they weren't — because the kit was the only thing in the band not coming out of a speaker.

          That's the actual diagnosis, and it's a different problem from the one it looks like. Everything else — both guitars, both vocals — arrived at the audience at full strength from two boxes at the front of the stage. The kit arrived acoustically, from well upstage, having lost level the whole way. From where you were standing it sounded balanced. Twenty feet out it wasn't close. Outdoors it's worse: last time a back wall at least bounced some of the kit forward, and this time nothing will.

          Two independent fixes, and you're doing both this time:

          Put the kit in the PA
          That's what the six mics are for. Once kick and snare are coming out of the same speakers as everything else, they arrive with the same authority instead of fighting a distance handicap. This is the main fix.
          Move the kit downstage
          Cheap and worth doing anyway. Distance costs roughly 6 dB every time it doubles, and in open air there's no wall or ceiling to give any of it back. Get him as far forward as the stage allows.

          Worth knowing what to expect: the mics won't make the kit louder on stage — it's already the loudest thing up there. What they do is make the kick punch and the snare crack land out front. So don't chase "more drums" by pushing all six faders up. Push kick and snare; the toms and overheads are seasoning.

          And your monitoring problem was a separate thing entirely — no wedge, so you were stepping out in front of the PA to hear anything. The Zone/Sub feed solves that one, and it means you'd have caught this drum problem yourself last time.

          Placement, in order of how much it matters

          Match the overhead distances. This is the big one.
          Use a mic cable as a tape measure: run it from the centre of the snare to one overhead capsule, pinch it, then check the other one is the same length. If the distances don't match, the snare arrives at each mic at a different moment and partially cancels — and a mic'd kit that sounds smaller than the acoustic kit is almost always this. Also keep each overhead at least three times as far from the snare as the snare mic is (the 3:1 rule).
          Angle the snare mic away from the hi-hat
          1–2" in from the rim, over the top head, pointed at the centre, back turned to the hats. There are no gates on this board, so whatever the snare mic hears is in the mix all night.
          Kick mic at the beater, not the middle
          Two to four inches inside the port, aimed at the spot where the beater strikes. That's where click comes from — you're spending the Mid on a 375 Hz cut instead, so placement has to supply the attack.
          Check the tom clips against his swing
          Rim clips put the mic right in the stick path. Have him play the fills he actually plays before you settle them, not a polite roll.
          SM57 halfway out on the Katana's cone
          Right up on the grille, an inch or two off, aimed halfway between the dust cap and the edge. Dead centre is bright and harsh, the edge is dark, halfway is the safe start.

          If you run short on time or headroom

          Pull the tom mics and one overhead. Kick, snare and a single centred overhead is a completely legitimate three-mic kit and it's far harder to get wrong than six mics. Six open drum mics on a loud stage with no gates is a lot of accumulated bleed, and bleed is what turns a mix to mud. Add the toms back only if the kit still has room to breathe.

          Gain staging & ringing outSet Level LED, the VU meter and the Equalizer

          Gain staging, properly

          Every channel has a green Set Level LED and a red Clip LED next to its Gain knob, so you don't have to guess. Solo the channel, ask for their loudest, and raise Gain until Set Level flashes on the peaks — that's the panel's own mark for correct gain. Channels 1–10 have a built-in compressor that can't be switched off and is driven by the Gain knob: Set Level lit solid means it's working, which is fine in small doses and a sign to back off if it never lets go. Clip lights 3 dB before real clipping. Do all of it with the channel fader down. Gain sets how hard the signal hits the board; the fader sets how much of it goes out front.

          Ringing out, outdoors

          With nothing to bounce off, the mains ring far less outside than in a room — the limit you hit first is running out of level, not feedback. The wedges are the likely ringers, your Zone/Sub wedge most of all. With the band quiet, open Vox 1 and slowly push MAIN until something starts to ring. Find it on the Equalizer, pull that slider 3–6 dB, and stop after one or two notches. Past three you're making the PA sound worse than the feedback was.

          Identifying the ring by ear: a low howl is 125–250 Hz; a hollow "oooo" is 500 Hz–1 kHz; a hard ring is 2–4 kHz; a piercing whistle is 8 kHz and up.

          The drummer's wedgeMON out into the Kustom FX return

          Straight into the FX Return, so the vocals never touch a guitar preamp. That's the right call, and it changes what you can adjust once you're playing. Outdoors it matters more: he gets no room sound to lean on, so his wedge is all he hears.

          What the FX Return actually gets you

          It lands after the preamp and the whole tone stack, essentially just hitting the power amp and speaker. No guitar voicing, no preamp colour on the vocals — which is the entire point. The trade is that the amp's Volume, Bass, Mid, Treble and DFX knobs are all upstream of where you're plugging in, so none of them do anything. The drummer has no controls at all. Your MON fader is his only volume, and you have no EQ on his feed either, since the Equalizer is assigned to Main.

          So you shape his mix with send levels, not EQ

          This is the part that changes with the tone stack out of the picture. A 100-watt guitar speaker has no useful low end, and anything low you send it is wasted power and flap. Your HPFs are already doing most of that work — Mon is tapped after the channel EQ and HPF, so every channel with its filter in arrives already cleaned up. The one channel that doesn't is the bass, so keep its Mon send moderate rather than maxed and let the 800 Hz boost on that channel carry it. Upper harmonics are what survive a guitar speaker anyway; he'll hear the bass line fine.

          Do this and it works twice as well

          Put the amp on a chair or a road case, aimed across the kit at his head. This matters more than ever going into the FX Return: you get a fixed amount of power and no way to add gain at the amp, so the only lever left is getting the sound to his ears instead of his shins. Aiming it across the kit rather than up also keeps vocal bleed out of the overheads, and outdoors it keeps the wind from carrying it off.

          If it isn't loud enough

          With the MON fader up and the channel sends up, what you've got is what you've got — there's no gain stage left at the amp. If that ceiling is too low, the fallback is moving the cable to the Rhythm channel's guitar input, which has preamp gain and hands the drummer his own Volume back. You lose some clarity to the preamp, so on the amp keep Bass well down, Mid a little past halfway, Treble at halfway, the boost switch off and DFX at zero. Try the FX Return first; for cue-level vocals it should be plenty.

          Set expectations with him either way: it will sound thin. It's a guitar speaker rolling off somewhere above 5 kHz. It's a cue wedge, not a mix. Vocals and bass will be audible and that is the entire job.

          Where every output goesSignal flow, Zone/Sub, MON and Aux

          This board has more routing than it looks like from the front: three separate send buses per channel plus a fourth output taken off the main mix. Both monitors are covered and you still have a spare.

          The move that fixes your monitoring

          The Zone/Sub output is a mono sum of the main mix, taken after the MAIN fader, with its own level knob on the top panel and its jack on the rear. Send it to your wedge and you are hearing exactly what the crowd is getting, at whatever volume you want, with nothing to set up and nothing to keep in sync. Last gig you had no wedge at all and were stepping out in front of the PA to check the mix — this is the fix, and it costs you zero channels and zero sends.

          Why the drummer gets MON and not Aux

          Mon is permanently pre-fader, which is exactly what you want for a cue mix — nothing you do to the main mix can disturb it. Aux is the switchable one, which makes it the more flexible spare. MON also has a dedicated fader in the master section, so the drummer's overall level is a single fader you can find without looking, instead of a small knob. That matters a lot given he has no volume control of his own.

          Why you get Zone/Sub and not a real send

          Zone/Sub is the only output that mirrors the main mix automatically. Any of the three sends would mean building a second mix by hand and then keeping it in sync with the first one all night, while playing guitar. Zone/Sub costs nothing and is more accurate than anything you'd build by hand.

          The Equalizer serves one bus or the other

          The 9-band Equalizer has an Assign switch: Main or Mon. It cannot do both. Set it to MAIN — the mains are what the audience hears, and outdoors that 125 Hz lift is doing real work. The consequence is that the drummer's wedge gets no graphic EQ at all, which matters more than usual now that you're going into the FX Return. The drummer section covers how to shape his feed without it.

          Three things to know before you're standing there

          Zone/Sub is after MAIN
          Pull MAIN down between songs and your wedge dies with it. Use the Zone/Sub knob, not MAIN, when you want your own level to change. BREAK mutes the inputs, so your wedge goes quiet between sets too.
          Your wedge EQ lives on the speaker
          With the Equalizer assigned to Main, you have no board EQ on the Zone/Sub feed. The YXL10P has a Monitor voicing mode on its back panel — that's your wedge EQ, and it's built for exactly this.
          Check the Zone/Sub tap in ten seconds at soundcheck
          Sources disagree on whether Zone/Sub is taken before or after the Equalizer. Pull one slider to the floor and listen to your wedge: if it changes, Zone/Sub is after the Equalizer and your wedge follows the main EQ. Either way it works, but it's worth knowing which.
          Input listMics, jacks, stands, phantom

          Every mic lands on a mono channel, because only the mono channels have the sweepable Mid and the compressor. Your guitar goes on the stereo strip 11/12 through its L/MONO jack. Every channel on this board has the 80 Hz high-pass switch.

          ChSourceMic / DIIntoStand48VHPF

          Phantom power is one switch on the rear panel

          There's no per-channel phantom: one 48 V switch on the back puts phantom on every XLR input. The "48V" column is only which mics need it. Harmless to the SM57s, SM58s and Apex dynamics — all balanced. Two rules anyway: flip it only with MAIN down, and never plug or unplug a mic while it's live. This is also why your G3Xn goes in on a 1/4" jack, not XLR — an unbalanced line source on a phantom-powered XLR input is asking for trouble.

          If the bassist's amp turns out to have an XLR DI out, use it instead of the SM57 — more consistent, and it frees a mic and a stand. Engage its ground lift if it has one.

          Stage plotKit downstage, mains in front of the mics

          Two things this drawing exists to show: the mains sit downstage of every vocal mic, and the kit comes forward from where it was last time.

          Bring the kit downstage
          This is the direct fix for last gig. Put the drummer as far forward as the stage and the band will tolerate — every doubling of distance costs about 6 dB, and in open air nothing bounces back to make up for it. The mics do most of the work now, but free level is still free.
          Mains high on the SKS-09B stands
          Tweeter roughly at standing head height, aimed at the crowd. Up there the sound clears the front row instead of cooking it, and outdoors it carries further back.
          Weigh the stands down
          A gust will tip a speaker on a stand. Sandbag or strap the legs, and keep the legs out of the walkway.
          Your wedge angled up at your head
          On the floor at your feet. If the YXL10P has a monitor angle moulded into one side, lay it on that side. Set its voicing mode to Monitor.
          Put your SM58's dead spot on the wedge
          A cardioid SM58 is deafest directly behind itself. Face the mic with the wedge between you and the audience firing back up at you, and the wedge sits in the null. That geometry is what lets a wedge get loud at all.
          Mixer at your position, in the shade
          On a table or a road case beside you, within arm's reach — not out front, and not in full sun. You're mixing while playing; if you can't reach it between songs it may as well be in the van.
